\section{Introduction}
% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Financial sentiment analysis on social media has practical applications in algorithmic trading, market surveillance and investor-sentiment tracking. Unlike general-purpose sentiment analysis, financial tweets present unique challenges: domain-specific vocabulary (cashtags, financial jargon), extreme brevity and an inherent class imbalance in which neutral commentary dominates.

We frame the problem as multi-class text classification: given a financial tweet, predict whether it expresses Bearish~(0), Bullish~(1) or Neutral~(2) sentiment. Our approach evaluated 32~combinations of feature-extraction methods and classifiers using 5-fold stratified cross-validation, with macro-F1 as the primary metric.

\begin{sloppypar}
The best pipeline --- Twitter-RoBERTa embeddings combined with XGBoost --- achieves a macro-F1 of \textbf{0.794}, outperforming traditional approaches by a substantial margin and demonstrating that domain-aligned pretrained representations matter more than classifier sophistication.
\end{sloppypar}

\section{Data Exploration}
\label{sec:data}
% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

The dataset comprises 9,543~labeled training tweets and 2,388~unlabeled test tweets across three sentiment classes. It exhibits a strong class imbalance: Neutral tweets (64.7\%) outnumber Bearish tweets (15.1\%) by a factor of $4.3\times$ (Table~\ref{tab:class_dist}). Two consequences shape the rest of the pipeline: (i)~we use \texttt{class\_weight='balanced'} in every classifier, and (ii)~we adopt macro-F1 rather than accuracy as the primary metric, since a naive all-Neutral classifier would already reach 64.7\% accuracy while failing entirely on the minority classes.

\centering
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Class} & \textbf{Count} & \textbf{Proportion} \\
\midrule
Bearish (0) & 1,442 & 15.1\% \\
Bullish (1) & 1,923 & 20.1\% \\
Neutral (2) & 6,178 & 64.7\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Training-set class distribution.}
\label{tab:class_dist}
\end{table}

Tweet lengths range from 2 to 48~tokens (median:~18), and all three classes share similar length distributions (Figure~\ref{fig:tweet_length}), so length itself is not discriminative. This also justifies our \texttt{max\_length=128} setting for the Transformer tokenizers --- no tweet approaches that limit.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{tweet_length_distribution.png}
\caption{Tweet-length distribution across sentiment classes.}
\label{fig:tweet_length}
\end{figure}

Financial tweets carry distinctive surface markers: \$TICKER cashtags appear in ${\sim}$60\% of tweets, @mentions in ${\sim}$15\% and URLs in ${\sim}$20\%. Cashtags carry real contextual signal, but overfitting to specific tickers would not generalise, so we replace them with a \texttt{<TICKER>} placeholder.

Analysis of the most frequent tokens per class (Figure~\ref{fig:vocab}) reveals clear thematic separation: Bearish tweets feature words such as ``cut'', ``downgrade'' and ``sell''; Bullish tweets contain ``buy'', ``upgrade'' and ``breakout''; Neutral tweets use informational vocabulary (``earnings'', ``report'', ``market'') that overlaps with both sentiment classes, making the Neutral boundaries the primary source of classification error.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{top_tokens_per_class.png}
\caption{Top tokens per class after light cleaning.}
\label{fig:vocab}
\end{figure}

Data-quality checks found fewer than 1\% exact duplicates (retained, to avoid biasing the class proportions) and no label leakage: ``bearish'' and ``bullish'' appear naturally in financial discourse and represent legitimate patterns rather than annotation shortcuts.

\section{Data Preprocessing}
\label{sec:preprocessing}
% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\begin{sloppypar}
Preprocessing was implemented as a set of composable functions in \texttt{src/preprocessing.py}, which we combine into three named pipelines and benchmark systematically.
\end{sloppypar}

\subsection{Preprocessing Functions}

Each cleaning step is a standalone function so that pipelines can be assembled and ablated:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{HTML unescape} --- decode \texttt{\&amp;}, \texttt{\&lt;}, \dots
    \item \textbf{URL removal} --- strip \texttt{http://} and \texttt{www.} links.
    \item \textbf{Mention removal} --- strip \texttt{@username} handles.
    \item \textbf{Hashtag handling} --- strip the leading \texttt{\#} but keep the word.
    \item \textbf{Ticker normalisation} --- replace cashtags with \texttt{<TICKER>}.
    \item \textbf{Number normalisation} --- percentages\,$\rightarrow$\,\texttt{<PCT>}, numbers\,$\rightarrow$\,\texttt{<NUM>}.
    \item \textbf{Emoji demojisation} --- convert emojis to text descriptions, preserving sentiment signal.
    \item \textbf{Unicode NFKC} --- consistent encoding of look-alike characters.
    \item \textbf{Lowercasing} --- standard case folding.
    \item \textbf{Punctuation collapse} --- \texttt{!!!}\,$\rightarrow$\,\texttt{!}; whitespace normalisation.
    \item \textbf{Finance-aware stopword removal} --- we keep negations (\texttt{no}, \texttt{not}, \texttt{nor}) and directional words (\texttt{up}, \texttt{down}, \texttt{over}, \texttt{under}); these are critical for sentiment polarity.
    \item \textbf{Lemmatisation~/~stemming} --- evaluated as optional token-level operations.
\end{enumerate}

We benchmarked the pipelines under a single fixed classifier (Logistic Regression on TF-IDF features) to isolate the preprocessing effect (Table~\ref{tab:pp_compare}).

\midrule
\texttt{pp\_minimal}              & \textbf{0.736} \\

Stopword removal strips negations such as ``not'' that flip sentiment, lemmatisation collapses temporal distinctions (``falling''\,$\rightarrow$\,``fall'') that carry urgency, and number normalisation discards specific values that provide context.

\section{Feature Engineering}
\label{sec:features}
% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

We implemented and compared eight feature-extraction methods across three families.

\subsection{Bag-of-Words Features}

\begin{sloppypar}
\textbf{CountVectorizer (BoW)}: unigram counts with a 10\,000-feature vocabulary cap.
\textbf{TF-IDF}: bigrams (\texttt{ngram\_range=(1,2)}), sublinear TF scaling and 20\,000~features with \texttt{min\_df=2} and \texttt{max\_df=0.95}.
TF-IDF outperformed BoW by 1--3~percentage points across all classifiers, confirming that local word order carries sentiment signal (Figure~\ref{fig:tfidf}).
\end{sloppypar}

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{top_tfidf_features.png}
\caption{Top TF-IDF features per class (Logistic Regression coefficients).}
\label{fig:tfidf}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Word Embeddings}

\textbf{Custom Word2Vec}: skip-gram, 100-d, trained on the financial tweet corpus, mean-pooled.
\textbf{GloVe Twitter 100-d}: pretrained on 2~billion tweets.
\textbf{TF-IDF--weighted pooling}: weights each token embedding by its TF-IDF score.

All Word2Vec variants underperform BoW/TF-IDF (macro-F1: 0.59--0.66 vs.\ 0.72--0.74). The primary reason is that mean pooling discards word order, which is critical for sentiment: averaged GloVe-Twitter vectors for ``stock is going up today'' and ``stock is going down today'' have a cosine similarity of \textbf{0.996} --- two sentences of opposite financial polarity, indistinguishable to any classifier downstream of the pooled representation.

\subsection{Transformer Encoders}

We used three pretrained Transformer encoders as frozen feature extractors: tokenise, pass through the encoder, mean-pool the last hidden states (768-d) with attention masking and feed the result to a downstream scikit-learn classifier. Embeddings are cached as \texttt{.npy} files, which keeps the workflow CPU-feasible.

\begin{table}[!htbp]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{llrl}
\toprule
\textbf{Encoder} & \textbf{Domain} & \textbf{Params} & \textbf{Status} \\
\midrule
DistilBERT       & General                 & 66M  & Mandatory \\
FinBERT          & Finance (SEC filings)   & 110M & Extra work \\
Twitter-RoBERTa  & Twitter + sentiment     & 125M & Extra work \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Transformer encoders evaluated as frozen feature extractors.}
\label{tab:transformers}
\end{table}

\begin{sloppypar}
\textbf{Key finding.} Domain alignment matters more than model size. DistilBERT (general-purpose, 66M~params) underperforms TF-IDF; Twitter-RoBERTa (tweet-domain, sentiment-tuned, 125M~params) achieves the best result overall; FinBERT sits between the two --- it understands financial language but was trained on formal SEC filings rather than informal tweets.

\textbf{Tokenisation.} The three encoders use WordPiece (DistilBERT, FinBERT) or byte-pair (Twitter-RoBERTa) vocabularies, so most finance vocabulary is split: \texttt{bullish}\,$\rightarrow$\,\texttt{bull|ish}, \texttt{downgrade}\,$\rightarrow$\,\texttt{down|grade}, \texttt{rebalancing}\,$\rightarrow$\,\texttt{re|bala|nc|ing} (BERT-family) or \texttt{re|bal|ancing} (RoBERTa). The \texttt{<TICKER>} placeholder is fragmented across all three tokenizers, so the encoder loses the holistic ``ticker'' token but still sees a consistent rare-token pattern across cashtags --- which is the property the placeholder was meant to preserve.

\textbf{Pooling.} The canonical sentence representation in BERT-family models is the \texttt{[CLS]} token. We used mean pooling and verified directly that this is not a performance compromise (Table~\ref{tab:pooling}): for the sentiment-tuned encoders the two pooling choices are within noise, and mean pooling slightly helps DistilBERT, whose \texttt{[CLS]} representation has not been fine-tuned for any sentiment task.
\end{sloppypar}

\subsection{Key Findings}

\textbf{Feature hierarchy} (most to least effective):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Twitter-RoBERTa} (0.794) --- pretrained on 58M~tweets with sentiment labels; complete domain alignment.
    \item \textbf{FinBERT} (0.779) --- finance-aware, but pretrained on formal text rather than tweets.
    \item \textbf{TF-IDF} (0.740) --- competitive: bigrams recover some local word order.
    \item \textbf{BoW} (0.720) --- solid unigram baseline.
    \item \textbf{DistilBERT} (0.705) --- general-purpose encoder underperforms TF-IDF, illustrating that domain alignment matters more than model sophistication.
    \item \textbf{Word2Vec variants} (0.658) --- mean pooling discards word order.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Classifier patterns.} XGBoost excels with dense Transformer embeddings (non-linear boundaries in 768-d space), while linear models (LogReg, SVM) excel with sparse features (BoW/TF-IDF), where regularisation handles high dimensionality well. Random Forest consistently underperforms both.

Standard deviations remain below 0.017, and the best configuration carries one of the lowest ($\pm$0.006) --- the ranking is stable across folds.

\subsection{Per-Class Analysis}

\begin{figure}[!htbp]

\subsection{Embedding Geometry}

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.66\textwidth]{pca_twitter_roberta.png}
\caption{PCA projection of Twitter-RoBERTa embeddings on the validation set (768-d $\rightarrow$ 2-d).}
\label{fig:pca}
\end{figure}

To check whether the best encoder genuinely separates the three classes in feature space, we projected the 768-d Twitter-RoBERTa embeddings of the validation set to 2-d via PCA (Figure~\ref{fig:pca}). PC1 alone accounts for 26.7\% of variance and roughly tracks the Bearish\,$\leftrightarrow$\,Bullish axis; Neutral occupies the centre of the cloud, mirroring the dominant error mode in the confusion matrices (Neutral\,$\leftrightarrow$\,sentiment confusion). The clusters overlap but remain visibly distinct --- this is the geometry that XGBoost exploits to reach 0.79~macro-F1.

\subsection{Error Analysis}

The misclassified tweets fall into three recurring patterns, visualised in the confusion matrices of Figure~\ref{fig:confusion}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Neutral\,$\leftrightarrow$\,Bearish}: tweets reporting negative news factually (e.g.\ ``Stock down 5\% after earnings miss'') are genuinely ambiguous between factual reporting and negative sentiment.
    \item \textbf{Neutral\,$\leftrightarrow$\,Bullish}: factual reports of positive events blur the line between informational and positive sentiment.
    \item \textbf{Bearish\,$\leftrightarrow$\,Bullish} (rarest): primarily sarcasm or contrarian positions.
\end{enumerate}

The dominant challenge is the fundamental ambiguity between factual reporting and sentiment expression in financial discourse.

\section{Extra Work}
\label{sec:extra}
% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\subsection{Domain-Specific Transformer Encoders}

\begin{sloppypar}
\extratag We evaluated two pretrained encoders beyond the mandatory DistilBERT. \textbf{FinBERT} (\texttt{ProsusAI/\allowbreak finbert}), trained on financial communications, reaches \textbf{0.779}~macro-F1 (a 7.4-pp improvement over DistilBERT). \textbf{Twitter-RoBERTa} (\texttt{cardiffnlp/\allowbreak twitter-roberta-\allowbreak base-sentiment-latest}), trained on 58M~tweets with sentiment labels, reaches \textbf{0.794}~macro-F1 --- the best overall.
\end{sloppypar}

\subsection{Zero-Shot Pretrained Classification}
\label{sec:zero_shot}

\extratag To isolate the contribution of the trained downstream classifier from that of the pretrained encoder, we applied Twitter-RoBERTa's built-in 3-class sentiment head (negative/neutral/positive) directly to the validation set, with no training of our own. Outputs were mapped to our label space: negative\,$\rightarrow$\,Bearish, positive\,$\rightarrow$\,Bullish, neutral\,$\rightarrow$\,Neutral.

The zero-shot baseline reaches \textbf{0.587}~macro-F1 (5-fold aggregation: $0.587 \pm 0.012$), substantially below the 0.794 obtained when the same encoder is paired with a trained XGBoost classifier (Table~\ref{tab:zero_shot}). The gap concentrates on the minority classes: Bearish/Bullish F1 fall from 0.65--0.75 (trained) to 0.48--0.49 (zero-shot), while Neutral degrades less. This reflects a label-space mismatch: a tweet using ``downgrade'', ``cut'' or ``miss'' is bearish in finance but not unambiguously negative in everyday speech. Supervised adaptation on $\sim$9.5k labeled examples is what recovers the missing $+0.21$~macro-F1; pretraining domain alignment is necessary but not sufficient.

\subsection{Agentic Workflow}

\begin{sloppypar}
\extratag A LangChain-based agent (\texttt{src/agent.py}) orchestrates five tools: \textit{classify\_tweet}, \textit{route\_model}, \textit{ensemble\_classify}, \textit{compare\_models} and \textit{explain\_prediction}. The agent inspects tweet content to choose a model, reconciles disagreements via majority vote and produces interpretable explanations from linear-model coefficients. Three illustrative transcripts (full logs in \texttt{outputs/\allowbreak agent\_transcripts.md}):
\end{sloppypar}

\textbf{Routing.} Input ``\$AAPL all-time high, breakout above resistance.'' --- the agent detects a \$TICKER, routes to FinBERT and returns Bullish (89.1\%).

\textbf{Ensemble.} Input ``Markets mixed; Fed signals rate changes amid inflation concerns.'' --- TF-IDF/LogReg, FinBERT and Twitter-RoBERTa vote Neutral while DistilBERT votes Bearish; the agent returns Neutral with 75\% agreement, illustrating how a general-purpose encoder can over-weight non-financial vocabulary.

\textbf{Attribution.} Input ``Sell everything, this stock is going to crash hard.'' --- LogReg coefficients identify ``sell'' ($+2.85$) and ``crash'' ($+2.63$) as the strongest Bearish signals, both carrying negative Bullish weights ($-1.92$, $-2.10$).

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

The systematic evaluation of 32~feature\,$\times$\,classifier combinations identifies \textbf{Twitter-RoBERTa\,+\,XGBoost} as the best pipeline (\textbf{0.794}~macro-F1). Four findings stand out:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Domain alignment outweighs model size.} DistilBERT (66M~params) underperforms TF-IDF bigrams; Twitter-RoBERTa (125M~params) exceeds both. Alignment alone is not enough either --- the same encoder, applied zero-shot (Section~\ref{sec:zero_shot}), reaches only 0.587~macro-F1; the trained head adds $+0.21$.
    \item \textbf{Feature extraction matters more than classifier choice.} The gap between feature families (0.60\,$\rightarrow$\,0.79) far exceeds the within-family gap (0.02--0.05).
    \item \textbf{Conservative preprocessing wins.} Aggressive normalisation removes sentiment signal; keeping negations and directional words intact is essential.
    \item \textbf{Class-imbalance handling is essential.} Without balanced class weights, classifiers default to predicting Neutral.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Limitations and future work.} The CPU-only constraint prevented full Transformer fine-tuning and made decoder inference infeasible; no hyperparameter optimisation was performed; results come from a single dataset. GPU fine-tuning, cross-family ensembles and temporal analysis of financial sentiment are the natural next steps.
